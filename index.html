Sherwood's Shrinking Footprint

At its medieval peak, Sherwood covered roughly 100,000 acres — about a fifth of Nottinghamshire. Today, the National Nature Reserve covers barely 450 hectares of ancient woodland.

The Central Conflict

Sherwood's history is a long struggle between overlapping claims — royal prerogative, aristocratic property, customary commons, and popular myth.

Forest Law & Royal Power

After 1066, Norman kings imposed a brutal Forest Law across a fifth of England. Sherwood's deer and timber were res nullius made royal — poaching meant blinding, castration, or death. The forest courts at Edwinstowe ran for centuries. When barons finally broke the king's will at Runnymede in 1215, forest abuses were a central grievance: Magna Carta included forest clauses, followed in 1217 by a dedicated Charter of the Forest.

Robin Hood & the Sheriff

The Robin Hood ballads (earliest written reference, c. 1377 in Piers Plowman) encode real grievances. Sheriffs collected taxes, enforced forest law, and frequently extorted the poor. Robin's "steal from the rich, give to the poor" is a folk memory of the dispossession that Forest Law created. The Sheriff of Nottingham embodies lawful-but-corrupt authority — not a villain outside the law, but the law itself abused.

Customary Commons

The commons were never free-for-all — they were intensely local systems of entitlement. Edwinstowe's poor exercised rights of firebote (firewood collection), gathering ferns to burn for soap-making lye, gleaning underwood, and feeding pigs on acorns. These rights, confirmed by custom and practice across generations, were the economic survival of the forest poor. They coexisted uneasily with Forest Law, persisting through the medieval period and into the 18th century.

Enclosure & Dispossession

By the 18th century, Crown land in Sherwood had been sold or gifted to nobles who created the "Dukeries" — Thoresby, Welbeck, Clumber, Rufford, and Newstead estates. Georgian "improvement" meant enclosure: common grazing land fenced off, customary rights extinguished, cottagers evicted. By 1830, even the last Crown lands at Bilhaugh and Birklands were sold. The 1818 Disafforestation Act formally ended Sherwood's status as a royal forest, completing a centuries-long privatisation.

Nottinghamshire County Council

Industry & Shrinkage

Industrialisation hit Sherwood from multiple directions: naval timber demands stripped mature oaks; coal mining opened the east Nottinghamshire coalfield, bringing colliery towns onto the forest margins; railways cut lines through woodland; Victorian urban expansion from Nottingham, Mansfield, and Worksop consumed the edges. WWI and WWII brought military requisition, ammunition stores, and prisoner-of-war camps. Softwood conifer plantations replaced native oak woodland in the 20th century under Forestry Commission management.

Edwinstowe Historical Society

Conservation & Rewilding

Since the 1970s, recognition of Sherwood's ecological uniqueness — highest density of ancient trees in Europe — has driven conservation. Designated SSSI in 1981 and National Nature Reserve in 2002, the remnant 450 ha Birklands/Bilhaugh complex holds over 1,000 veteran oaks. The Sherwood Forest Trust and partners are leading replanting and heathland restoration, removing conifer plantations and working toward a more ecologically connected landscape. The 2007 Major Oak replanting symbolised this renewal.
